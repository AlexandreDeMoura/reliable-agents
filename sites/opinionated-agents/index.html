Agents You Can Sign Off On
A hands-on guide to reliable agents

Agents you can sign off on

An agent is useful to a whole organization only when people trust its work the way they trust a colleague's. This guide shows what that takes: Opinions written down, and ways to check that the agent holds them.

Chapter 00

Faster people, same company

Give one person a capable agent and their own output can grow tenfold or more. Look at a whole company and most of that gain disappears.

This is strange, because companies already know how to get good work from workers who don't behave the same way every time: their people. They do it with a Process. Agents usually arrive without the agent-side equivalent, which is a set of Opinions.

Try it on a small firm of twelve preparers. Hand out agents and watch two numbers: what the firm produces, and what its reviewers can sign off on.

Twelve preparers, twelve agentsIllustrative numbers

Each square is one piece of work a month. Reviewers can check 16 pieces a month. Work done the firm's way passes. Work an agent did its own way gets checked in full, and wrong pieces go back. Preparers who steer well get far more done. The firm barely moves, and with everyone on their own agent it signs off on less than before.

Chapter 01

How organizations trust people

Picture an accounting firm. The head of the firm has no idea what a preparer three levels down does on a Tuesday. New clients arrive every month: a dental practice, a SaaS startup, a vineyard. Yet the head of the firm trusts the books that go out.

That trust doesn't come from people being predictable. It comes from a Process the firm signed off on, chosen for its risk tolerance, its work and its clients. If the people who set the Process believe it leads to good outcomes, they can trust the result, even though the people following it are not perfectly predictable.

The Process goes all the way down. Inside the month-end close, reconciling cash is one step. Inside that, getting the bank data is one step. Even that small step has a fixed way of doing it.

Chapter 02

Two reasons agents don't spread through a company

Most agent products hand the hard part to the user. You decide which guidance the agent loads, what it remembers, when to use it and when not to. To do this well, you need a working sense of how the agent thinks. A few people are good at this. Most knowledge workers are not, and shouldn't have to be.

Steering burden. The skill a user needs to get good work from an agent: managing its Context and knowing when to use it.

The second problem shows up even when every user is skilled. When each person shapes their own agent, the way the work gets done depends on whoever happens to be using it. That runs against how organizations scale. The way work is done should come from the people responsible for it, not be reinvented by each person.

Opinion gap. The lack of shared Opinions when each user shapes the agent their own way, so the same work comes out differently from user to user.

Remove both problems and you get the two properties that matter. The agent is simple: you say what you want, as you would to a colleague, and it works, without special skill. The agent is reliable: it works the same way across every Situation it meets.

Chapter 03

Reliable means consistent, not perfect

Nobody expects perfect work, from people or from agents. What a firm needs is work done the same way each time. If the agent can't handle something, it should fail at it every time, so you know where to look. Then it improves, the way a new colleague does.

An agent that is excellent one day and misses something basic the next is not reliable, however good its average.

Agent B is the one a firm can work with. Its mistakes sit in one place, so you can send scanned statements to a reviewer, trust the rest, and fix that one place next. Agent A's mistakes move every time, so every piece of its work has to be checked.

The way to get there is to be opinionated: decide how the agent should do each thing, write it down as an Opinion, and have the agent do it that way every time.

Chapter 04

Opinions: one chosen way, every time

Take a task that sounds simple: read a bank statement PDF. There are many ways to do it. Send it to a vision model. Write code around an open-source OCR library. Use a specialist OCR provider. Run OCR and have a vision model check the result. Run five models and compare. The list doesn't end.

Ask an agent to "extract this PDF" and it picks one way. The result may well be good, but it won't be reliable. No single method is the problem. The problem is that nobody chose.

Opinion. A deliberately chosen way of doing something, decided by people and written down so the agent does it the same way every time.

One statement, six extractionsIllustrative
Statement
Same file, six extractions

Many Opinions don't need a study. They come from expertise and first principles. An agent that reads the primary source before reaching a tax conclusion is more likely to be right. An agent that looks at the rendered slide deck before handing it over is less likely to ship a formatting mistake. You know both before writing a single Outcome eval. Where you do have Outcome evals, they show what actually works.

Opinions are not only about accuracy. They also settle trade-offs: latency, cost, what the model can do, and what you're willing to accept. Looking at every slide adds time. If formatting mistakes are rare enough for you, skipping the look is a perfectly good Opinion. What matters is that someone chose.

Choosing an Opinion for slide decksIllustrative numbers

An Opinion also has a reach. A team that only ever reads bank statements can afford an approach built for bank statements. A team that has to read any PDF needs a general approach, like the flow above. Match the reach to the Situations the agent will face.

Chapter 05

Where Outcome evals stop

An Outcome eval checks the final result of a Trajectory against Ground truth. Outcome evals are valuable, and they are scarce: a team might have a hundred, perhaps a thousand.

Production is far bigger. Real work brings hundreds of thousands of Situations, and the number keeps growing as agents take on longer work.

So how do you know your Outcome evals say anything about the rest? You can't prove each Trajectory right, because most have no Ground truth. But you can look at each Trajectory and check whether the Behavior you want is there. If it holds across the spread of Situations, the agent is more likely to generalize. That isn't proof, but it is Signal you can collect on every Trajectory.

Chapter 06

What shapes a Behavior

Back to the $412.06. The firm holds one clear Opinion: when a reconciliation doesn't match, the agent investigates and explains the difference, and never posts an entry only to make it match. In this Trajectory, the agent did the opposite.

Where did that come from? Not from one sentence. In a large agent, a Behavior comes from all of the Context at once: standing instructions, guidance revealed along the way and tool descriptions, plus whatever the model picked up in training. Two lessons follow. First, treat every word of the Context as data the agent learns from while it works. Each word shapes Behavior, and so does what is missing.

Second, you can't write every Behavior you want into the Context explicitly. There are too many, and they interact. What you need is a short, separate list of the Behaviors that matter most, and a way to check them on every Trajectory. That list is the subject of chapter 7.

Chapter 07

Behavior specs and the Judge

Chapter 6 left a problem. You can't write every Behavior into the Context, and you can't count on the Context alone to produce them. So write the most important ones down somewhere else.

Behavior spec. A short written list of the most important Opinions an agent must hold, used both to align a team and as a grading checklist.

The first job comes before anyone edits the Context. The builders agree on what they want, one Opinion at a time. The spec sits apart from the Context: the Context is what the agent reads, and the spec is what you want it to do. Keep it short. List only the Opinions that matter most, because listing everything just repeats the Context.

The second job comes after. For any Trajectory, with or without Ground truth, ask two questions. Did the agent reach a Situation where this Behavior applies? If so, did it show the Behavior? That check is a Behavior eval, and a model that performs it is a Judge.

Every Trajectory can get a Behavior eval, in Outcome evals and in production alike, because none of it needs an answer key. That gives you far more Signal than writing more Outcome evals ever could. Outcome evals still matter, but they grow one Ground truth at a time.

A Judge can be wrong too. That's why it quotes its evidence: a person can confirm a result in seconds without rereading the whole Trajectory.

Chapter 08

Forks: go back to the moment it went wrong

A Behavior eval tells you that a Behavior broke. It doesn't tell you why. A long Trajectory can hold a thousand model calls over several hours. Reading it end to end rarely shows the cause, and an Outcome eval tells you even less: it only says the final result was wrong.

Fork. A copy of a Trajectory taken at a chosen point, from which you can question the agent about its decision or change its Context and let it continue.

Forking on your own machine is easy. Forking a production agent is real engineering: the copy has to restore the environment, the data and the Context exactly as they were at that moment.

    Chapter 09

    Simulations: a cheap stand-in for a Fork

    Forks need infrastructure. A Simulation needs almost none, so you can use one today without building anything.

    Simulation. A helper agent role-playing the production agent with the same Context, used as a cheaper, rougher stand-in for a Fork.

    It sounds too simple to work, and it works well:

    1. Render the agent's full Context into files: standing instructions, guidance and tool descriptions, exactly as the agent sees them.
    2. Ask a coding agent to start helper agents.
    3. Each helper reads the files and plays your production agent. Tell it not to cheat.
    4. Describe a Situation, ideally one you saw in an Outcome eval or in production.
    5. See what it decides. If it reproduces the decision you saw, you can work with it.
    6. Ask why, and find the cause in the files. Change a file and ask again.
    Trying the tool fix across SituationsIllustrative

    A Simulation is far rougher than a Fork. The helper imagines the environment instead of living in it, so treat its answers as a quick first check while you change the Harness. Its strength is speed and cost: you can try ten ideas in the time a single Fork takes, then confirm the promising ones with a Fork.

    Chapter 10

    Closing the loop, earning trust

    Put the pieces together. Opinions, written down in a Behavior spec, let a Judge check every Trajectory. That gives you Signal, and Signal points to where an Opinion broke. Forks and Simulations find a fix and check it. The Harness improves, and sometimes the Opinion itself does. Then the agent works again, and the loop repeats.

    Without Opinions there is nothing to check a Trajectory against, so there is no Signal and no loop. Opinions work like a reward function one level up: they say what "better" means. Once that is written down, agents themselves can run more and more of this loop.

    Going around the loopIllustrative numbers

    The loop is also how trust builds. Think about how you review a colleague's pull request. You weigh two things: how much could break if it's wrong, and how much you trust the person. A new colleague's change to a core service gets read line by line. Your most trusted engineer's edit to an internal document gets a glance.

    People treat agent work the same way. An agent starts on narrow, low-risk work. As Behavior evals keep holding, people trust it more and review it less, and it takes on work where more could break. The head of the firm ends up trusting agent work the way they trust their people's work, through a Process they believe in and evidence that it is followed.

    Chapter 11

    Won't Opinions block a better way?

    A fair objection: left free, an agent might find a better way, a surprising and brilliant move like AlphaGo's move 37 against Lee Sedol in 2016. It might. But that isn't how an organization gets reliable work done at scale. Nobody wants a surprising, brilliant move in their tax return. They want it done reliably, at scale, in a way they can trust and that their people know how to work with.

    Opinions aren't frozen, either. As models improve, Opinions move up a level. A weaker model needs the exact method spelled out. A stronger one can be trusted with the method, as long as what matters still holds and is still checked.

    That's the whole idea. For agents to spread through a company, they have to be simple. For teams to trust them, they have to be reliable. Opinions give you both, and they make the Signal possible that lets agents improve in production.
